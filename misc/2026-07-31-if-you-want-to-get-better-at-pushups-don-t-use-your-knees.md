Working out doesn't come easy to everyone. Pure physical ability isn't a uniform quality, and while just about every person who walks through a set of gym doors can train to become bigger, faster, and stronger, assuming that the starting point will be the same no matter who you are is misguided at best and delusional—and potentially harmful–at worst. One of the most fundamental exercises, the pushup, is a perfect example of this. Some beginner trainees will immediately have the ability to pump through reps with solid form after a bit of basic coaching, while others will struggle to complete a single one. The key for that latter group, then, is building up enough strength to be able to confidently (and correctly) complete a set of pushups without flagging.

So that trainees who struggle with standard versions of exercises can build the strength they need to progress, trainers often depend on regressions. These are modifications of the exercises that are adjusted to be less challenging, often by shifting the position to one that's more mechanically advantageous (even if it's less effective for building muscle and strength).

The most common regression most trainers suggest for the pushup is for exercisers to lower down from the high plank position onto their knees. This makes it much easier to finish a rep, since the person is working with a lighter load from their knees to their shoulders rather than their feet to their shoulders—but it also takes away one of the pushup's key components.

Let's break down what it takes to do a good pushup, and the one simple regression that you can use to build strength to do them the right way.

Why Pushups Are a Great Exercise

We love pushups. The bodyweight move is one of the first exercises just about everyone learns as a kid because they're so very simple: all you do is lower yourself down, then push yourself back up. Pump through enough reps, and you can build a seriously strong chest and arms.

Sure, once you've grown a bit and you actually have some goals for your training, the move is just a bit more complicated than just going down and up. You need to be able to own your plank position to start, keeping your spine neutral and core engaged. Then, you can't just drop yourself to the floor, pelvis first. Doing a good pushup requires you to control your descent so that you lower your chest just above the ground to a point where you could hold for more than just a second if need be, then extending your arms to return all the way up to the top of the position before moving on to the next rep. Need to see that play out in real time? Watch and learn below.

●Get into a high plank position, with your hands stacked below your shoulders and your feet together.

●Squeeze your glutes and core tightly. Your butt shouldn't rise above your shoulders; try to form a straight line from your shoulders to your feet.

●Look down at the ground below you to keep your neck in a neutral position. Squeeze your shoulder blades to create mid-back tension.

●Bend your elbows to lower your torso down to the ground. Keep your elbow close to the torso; avoid flaring them out.

●Press off the ground to fully extend your elbows back up to the top position.

So if you want to try a workout that you're not ready for strength-wise, one option is to scale the exercises with regressions. Again, the most common way that trainers scale the pushup is to perform reps on their knees; some trainers even build knee pushups into their programming for beginners. While this isn't going to hurt you, necessarily, it's not going to do much to help you, either.

Hit the Bench for Better Pushups

Instead, your best bet to build real strength while working your way up to a full pushup (or your regression when you can't keep performing reps with proper form during a workout) is to elevate your hands on a raised surface to do incline pushups. If you're in a gym setting, a bench works well—but you can use an ottoman, couch, or even steps if you're at home, too.

"We see that a lot [knee pushups]—and then we see people to struggle to evolve to the full pushup. That's because you're not training your glutes and core to properly stabilize," says Men's Health fitness director Ebenezer Samuel, C.S.C.S.

Rather than focusing on taking your abs and legs out of the equation, focus instead on engaging them even more.

"Instead of doing starting your pushups on your knees, place your hands on a bench and gradually progress to using lower and lower benches (or whatever platform you have handy) for your hands. This will let you work with less load but still train core tension," Samuel advises.

So next time your pushup form starts to flag, look to raise up instead of dropping down. Follow these easy steps:

●Get into a high plank position with your hands stacked on the raised surface below your shoulders and your feet together.

●Keep your neck in a neutral position by looking straight ahead. Squeeze your shoulder blades to create mid-back tension.

●Bend your elbows to lower your torso down to the raised platform. Keep your elbow close to the torso; avoid flaring them out.
