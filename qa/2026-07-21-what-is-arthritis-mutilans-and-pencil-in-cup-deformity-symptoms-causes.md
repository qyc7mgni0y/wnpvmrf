Over time, arthritis mutilans leads to the destruction of the affected bones in your hands, particularly in your fingers. It can also affect your feet. People with arthritis mutilans may eventually develop short, unstable fingers, sometimes described by nicknames like “opera glass hands” or “telescoping fingers.”

[2]

Signs and Symptoms of Arthritis Mutilans

Arthritis mutilans is marked by the breakdown and shortening of bones in the fingers and, sometimes, toes. Other areas of the body can be involved, too.

[3]

Shortened fingers or toes

Unstable “flail,” or hypermobile, joints in fingers or toes

Difficulty using hands in daily tasks

Difficulty walking due to foot involvement

Telescoping fingers, with loose skin that can be pulled out

Severe joint destruction in hands or feet, as seen in X-rays

Neck or back pain

Changes in the structure of your hands or feet caused by arthritis mutilans are permanent and tend to develop over a long period of time — from many years to decades.

How Is Arthritis Mutilans Diagnosed?

Your doctor will diagnose arthritis mutilans based on a physical exam of your hands and feet, as well as X-rays that can show underlying degeneration of bones and joints.

Having symptoms of psoriatic arthritis for a long time

Unstable flail joints in fingers or toes

Treatment of Arthritis Mutilans

There is no special treatment for arthritis mutilans, outside of treatments for psoriatic arthritis in general. Once you develop the structural changes in your hands or feet that define arthritis mutilans, those changes cannot be reversed — but it’s possible that further damage to your bones and joints may be slowed or prevented.

[9]

Over-the-counter painkillers like ibuprofen (Advil, Motrin) or acetaminophen (Tylenol)

Heat or cold therapy

Physical or occupational therapy
